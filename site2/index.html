How a job runs

Four steps. You approve a fixed price before anything is manufactured, and nothing is added to it afterwards.

Step 1

You send the part

A CAD file, a drawing, a photo of the broken original, or a description of what it has to do. No file is fine.

Step 2

Quote and design notes

Within 2 business days: fixed price, material and tolerance recommendation, and anything in the design likely to fail in service.

Step 3

Manufacture and check

Made, then measured against the dimensions and fits we agreed. Critical holes are test-fitted, not assumed.

Step 4

Packed and shipped

Packed for the part and sent by courier, with tracking. Shipping cost is in the quote, never added at the end.

A dimensional check with every order

Key dimensions measured against what we agreed, and the result written down. If something is out, you hear it from me before it ships.

Engineering decisions come before manufacturing decisions

A print service takes your file and runs it. This works the way a part is released in aviation or motorsport: decide what it has to survive, then decide how to make it.

Requirements first

Where it mounts, what it carries, how hot it gets, whether anyone has to service it, and what happens if it fails. That conversation comes before any print file.

Fits and interfaces

Critical clearances, fasteners, assembly order and inspection points are agreed up front, so the part works in your assembly and not only on the bench.

Material and process

Filament, print orientation, wall count and reinforcement are chosen against the operating case, balancing mass, stiffness, heat resistance, finish and cost.

Choosing the material

Most parts that fail were printed in the wrong plastic, not printed badly. If you tell me where the part lives and what it carries, this choice is mine to get right — but here is the reasoning, so you can check it.

Material Useful up to Character Outdoors & UV Where it belongs
PLA ~50 °C Stiff but brittle. Snaps rather than bends. Poor Indoor jigs, mock-ups, fit checks, display parts. Never in a car in summer.
PETG ~70 °C Tough, forgiving, slight flex before it breaks. Moderate The default for general functional parts, enclosures and brackets that live indoors.
ABS ~90 °C Tough and machinable. Can be vapour smoothed and glued solvent-tight. Poor unless painted Car interior trim, engine-bay-adjacent parts kept out of sunlight.
ASA ~95 °C Like ABS, but it survives sunlight. Good Anything permanently outdoors: drone shells, exterior trim, sensor housings.
PC ~110 °C Very strong and stiff, high impact resistance. Moderate High-load, high-temperature brackets where a metal part would be overkill.
TPU ~70 °C Flexible, rubbery, absorbs vibration. Moderate Bushings, seals, grommets, drone landing feet, anti-vibration mounts.
PA-CF ~110 °C Stiffest option here. Low creep under sustained load, dimensionally stable. Moderate Structural drone brackets, arm clamps, fixtures that must hold shape under load.

Temperatures are practical service limits under light load, not datasheet peaks. Every printed part is weaker across the layers than along them, so orientation is chosen against the load direction, not for the fastest print. Where sunlight, fuel, solvents or sustained load are involved, say so in your enquiry — it changes the answer more than anything else.

Questions people ask before ordering

What will my part cost?
Use the estimator above for the order of magnitude. Small trim pieces, clips and knobs are usually €10–60; brackets, mounts and enclosures usually €40–150. Design work from a sketch or a broken original is quoted separately. You get one fixed price before anything is made, shipping included.
How long does it take?
Quote and design notes within 2 business days. Once you approve, most single parts are made within a few days. Larger runs and jobs needing CAD from scratch get a firm date in the quote.
Do you ship outside North Macedonia?
Yes. Domestic courier inside North Macedonia, regional courier to Serbia, Kosovo, Albania, Montenegro and Bosnia, and tracked delivery to EU countries. Shipping is quoted per order so you see the full cost before you commit.
What tolerances can you hold?
Around ±0.2 mm on general dimensions, and ±0.1 mm on critical features when we agree a test fit first. Bores, slots and press fits are checked against the mating part rather than trusted straight off the machine. If your part needs tighter than that, it needs machining, not printing, and I will tell you so.
Will a printed part really hold up?
In the right plastic, in the right orientation, for the right job — yes, and it has been doing so in cars, drones and machinery for years. In the wrong plastic it fails quickly and predictably: PLA in a hot car, any plastic loaded across its layers, an unfilleted inside corner. That judgement is the part of this you are actually paying for.
Is there a minimum order?
No. One part is a normal order. Small runs are welcome, and the per-part price drops with quantity.
Do I need a finished CAD file?
No. A photo with a ruler next to the part, a sketch with a few measurements, or the original part in your hand is enough to start. If you do have STEP, STL or DXF, send it and the quote comes back faster.
I only have the broken original. Now what?
Send photos first so I can check it is worth doing. If it is, the part is measured, redrawn in CAD, and improved where the original failed — thicker where it cracked, better fastener support where it pulled through. You get the part, and the CAD file if you want it.
Who owns the design?
You do. Drawings and CAD produced for your job are yours to use, and your files and photographs are not shown to anyone else or used as examples without your written permission.
Why source here instead of Western Europe or China?
For a hundred identical parts, you should not. For one part, a first prototype, or three revisions in a month, an overseas order means freight, customs and a week of waiting per revision. Here you get an engineer who reads the design, one courier hop, and a revision turned around in days.
